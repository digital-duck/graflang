REVISION_NEEDED
1. **Factual error in the CIA/ransomware example framing is fine, but the "Basic Cryptography" section mixes up digital signature and encryption roles in a way that could mislead.** Clarify that signatures are created with the private key over the hash and verified with the public key; the current text says the signer "encrypts that hash with a private key," which is acceptable but should say "signs" to avoid confusion with asymmetric encryption, which the same section defines differently.
2. **Hashing section overstates salt/slow-function guidance.** It says hashes "must be combined with a salt and a deliberately slow function such as bcrypt or Argon2" for password storage. This is the accepted practice, but the sentence should say "password hashing should use a salt and a deliberately slow, purpose-built function" so readers don't conclude that plain SHA-256 with a salt is acceptable for passwords.
3. **Version claim about NIST CSF 2.0 needs care.** "In version 2.0 it added an explicit Govern function" is correct, but the chapter should specify the year (2024) to be accurate and dated.
4. **Authentication section: "Passwords remain the most common factor" is an unsupported generalization.** Either cite a source or soften to "one of the most common."
5. **Attack surface sentence is slightly off.** "Every component adds to the surface" is an overstatement; components that are isolated and not exposed may add little. Suggest "components that are reachable or exposed add to the surface."
6. **Insider description is incomplete.** Insiders are described as "people with legitimate access," but the list omits that insiders can include contractors and partners with access; add that for completeness.
7. **Threat/risk definitions are sound, but the risk formula should note it is a conceptual model.** "Likelihood multiplied by impact" is a common simplification; a sentence noting that real risk assessment uses varied scoring methods would prevent readers from treating it as a precise calculation.
8. **Completeness gap: no mention of the CIA triad's extensions or the "security vs. safety" distinction, and no mention of logging/monitoring as a foundational principle**, though the defense-in-depth section gestures at monitoring. Consider a brief note on detection and logging, since later chapters on incident response depend on it.
9. **Minor clarity issue in the layered-controls example:** "A thief who defeats the fence still faces the lock" is fine, but the building analogy should note its limit (digital attackers can often reach many layers remotely at once), so readers don't over-trust the metaphor.
