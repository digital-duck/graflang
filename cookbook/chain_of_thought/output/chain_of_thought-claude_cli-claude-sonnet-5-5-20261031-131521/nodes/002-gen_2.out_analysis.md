# Analysis: Russia-Ukraine war research

The research is from training knowledge with a cutoff around June 2026, and today is 31 Oct 2026. Anything on diplomacy, front-line lines, and aid packages could be four or more months stale. Verify those against ISW, Reuters, or OHCHR before relying on them.

## Key trends

1. **The war has become attritional and technology-driven.** Drones make breakthroughs rare, so Russia's gains are slow and costly, mostly in Donetsk. Neither side is positioned for a decisive military outcome, which pushes the conflict toward a negotiated or frozen end state.
2. **The strike war is symmetric and escalating.** Russia targets cities and the grid, and Ukraine targets refineries, depots, and airbases deep in Russia. Each side is trying to raise the other's economic and political cost, which makes the war longer-term and less dependent on territory held.
3. **The burden of support is shifting to Europe.** US direct aid has shrunk, and Europe is covering financing and arms through loans and the frozen-asset debate. Russia's side is propped up by North Korean troops and munitions and by growing reliance on China.
4. **Diplomacy is active but stuck on the same four issues.** Territory (Donbas), security guarantees, Ukrainian army size, and sanctions relief have stayed unresolved through the 2025 Trump-led push.
5. **Both economies are on a war footing.** Russia faces inflation and labor shortages, and Ukraine faces a reconstruction bill of roughly $500bn+.

## Implications

- **A settlement is likely to be a bargain over leverage, not a victory.** Since neither side can win outright, the terms will depend on who can sustain costs longer. Watch Russian economic strain, European financing durability, and US commitment.
- **The frozen-asset decision matters beyond Ukraine.** Using roughly €200bn+ in Russian central bank assets would fund Ukraine for years, but it sets a precedent for reserve security and could affect how other countries hold assets in euros.
- **A deal that rewards conquest weakens the norm against changing borders by force.** That is the main spillover risk for Taiwan and other disputes. The security guarantees in any deal therefore matter as much as the territorial terms.
- **Europe is rearming for the long term.** NATO's 5% spending target, the Finland and Sweden accessions, and eastern-flank buildups point to a lasting shift whichever way the war ends.
- **The tail risks sit below open war.** Sabotage, drones, and cyber attacks on NATO states, plus nuclear signaling, make an accidental direct NATO-Russia incident the main escalation path.
- **The Russia-China-Iran-North Korea alignment is deepening.** This makes sanctions less effective over time and complicates Western strategy.

## Caveats on the research itself

- **The root-cause section is contested.** The realist view (NATO enlargement as trigger) and the revanchist view (Russian imperial aims, NATO as pretext) lead to different policy conclusions, so the summary shouldn't be read as settled.
- **Casualty figures range from hundreds of thousands to over a million.** That is too wide to support firm conclusions.
- **The territorial figure (about 19-20%) is a point-in-time number.** Check it against a current source.
