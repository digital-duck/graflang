## Challenges and Risks: Scalability, Talent, and Security Implications

The most immediate obstacle to useful quantum computing is scale, and it is harder than simply adding qubits. Running algorithms that matter commercially, such as breaking widely used public-key encryption or simulating complex catalysts, is estimated to require thousands of logical qubits. With current error-correction overheads, each logical qubit may consume hundreds to thousands of physical ones, which puts the total in the range of hundreds of thousands to millions of physical qubits. Reaching that figure raises engineering problems that do not appear in small devices. Superconducting systems need dilution refrigerators and dense cryogenic wiring that do not scale gracefully. Trapped-ion and neutral-atom machines face challenges in control-system complexity, gate speed, and keeping qubit quality uniform across large arrays. Photonic approaches must contend with loss and the difficulty of generating and detecting photons reliably. Across all platforms, qubit quality, connectivity, and the classical hardware that decodes errors in real time must improve together. Progress on any one dimension alone can be misleading. Cost and energy use add a further constraint, since a fault-tolerant machine may occupy a facility rather than a rack, and few organizations will be able to own one outright.

The second challenge is human. Quantum computing sits at the intersection of physics, computer science, materials science, and electrical engineering, and the pool of people who can work fluently across those fields is small. Employers report difficulty hiring for roles ranging from cryogenic and control engineering to compiler design and algorithm development, and universities are still building programs that produce graduates with this mix of skills. The shortage is not limited to researchers. As machines mature, the field will need technicians, software engineers, product managers, and domain specialists in chemistry, finance, and logistics who can judge which problems are actually suited to quantum methods. Competition for this talent is also geopolitical, with governments funding national programs and tightening export controls, which can fragment collaboration and make international hiring harder. Without sustained investment in education and in accessible tooling, the bottleneck may end up being people rather than hardware.

Security is the risk that has the shortest fuse, because it can arrive before the technology is fully capable. A sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor's algorithm would break RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, which protect most internet traffic, financial transactions, and digital signatures. The danger is already present in the form of "harvest now, decrypt later" attacks, in which adversaries collect encrypted data today and store it until they can decrypt it. Information that must stay confidential for decades, such as health records, state secrets, and long-lived intellectual property, is the most exposed. The response is migration to post-quantum cryptography, and standards bodies such as NIST have already published standardized algorithms. But migration is slow and unglamorous work. Organizations must inventory where cryptography is used, replace it in embedded devices and legacy systems, and design software to swap algorithms without a full rebuild, a property often called crypto-agility. The uncertainty over when a cryptographically relevant machine will exist argues for starting now rather than waiting for a firm date. Quantum technology also carries a promise on the defensive side, through quantum key distribution and quantum-secure communication, though these are narrower in practice and do not replace the broader shift to post-quantum algorithms.
