# Judgment: "AI will create more jobs than it eliminates"

## Verdict
**A narrow, low-confidence win for FOR.** Neither side meets the evidentiary burden the earlier rounds set. FOR has the stronger base-rate argument and a claim that matches its framing (net aggregate counts). AGAINST has the sharper structural argument, but it mostly rebuts a claim about distribution and transition that the resolution does not make.

## FOR: strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**
- **Point 1 (history)** uses accurate, well-sourced evidence. The Autor et al. finding that about 60% of 2018 job titles did not exist in 1940 is real and relevant. A two-century base rate is a legitimate prior.
- **Point 3 (task bundles)** is the most analytically useful argument. It explains why exposure is not elimination, and it cites a real study (Brynjolfsson, Li, Raymond, about 14% productivity gain). It also quietly rebuts AGAINST's reliance on IMF exposure figures.
- The causal chain in the conclusion (productivity, lower costs, new demand, new work) is coherent and has a long empirical record.

**Weaknesses**
- **The cognition-versus-muscle objection goes unanswered.** FOR never names task categories AI is unlikely to absorb, or explains why newly created job categories won't also be automatable. This is the decisive contested point, and FOR leaves it to the analogy.
- **The ATM example is incomplete.** Teller employment rose for decades but fell after about 2010. FOR uses it as proof without addressing the reversal, which weakens the "cheaper means more jobs" mechanism.
- **A productivity gain is not an employment gain.** The 14% result shows output per worker, not headcount. FOR treats the two as linked without evidence.
- **Points 4 and 5 are asserted, not shown.** There is no data that data-center or chip jobs are large relative to losses or durable rather than temporary construction. There is no data that solo founders net-hire. The WEF figures are employer-survey projections, not measurements.
- There is no post-2023 employment data, even though the debate is happening in late 2026.

## AGAINST: strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**
- **Point 1 (cognition)** is the best single argument in the debate. It identifies a real disanalogy: previous technologies left a cognitive refuge, and AI contests it.
- **Point 3 (entry-level erosion)** is a sharp, plausible structural claim. Aggregate counts can hide a broken apprenticeship ladder.
- **Point 4 (adjustment friction)** uses the China-shock literature accurately. Displaced workers did not smoothly reallocate.
- Point 5 correctly notes that the demand-elasticity mechanism is conditional, not guaranteed.

**Weaknesses**
- **The computerization precedent is ignored.** Typing, bookkeeping and switchboard work were cognitive and clerical, and were automated without net job loss. AGAINST's claim that this time is different needs an argument for why, and offers none beyond assertion.
- **The comparison is not like for like.** IMF exposure (which includes complemented jobs) is set against an unsourced gross creation figure ("thousands to low millions"). Exposure is not elimination.
- **Several claims are unevidenced.** These include that data-labeling jobs will be automated within a few years, that firms capture savings as margin rather than reinvesting, and that gains concentrate enough to suppress broad demand. "People don't buy ten times more legal summaries" is an assertion about elasticity with no sector data.
- **It argues past the resolution.** Point 4 effectively concedes that jobs may balance in aggregate ("the people eliminated are not the people hired"). That shows transition harm, not that the claim is false. The same goes for the quality and precarity arguments, since the claim reads as a count.
- **The freelance-market evidence is narrow.** Writing and translation postings are a small slice of the labor market, and they were shown only for the early post-ChatGPT period.
- **The burden-of-proof framing is rhetorical.** Declaring that the other side bears the burden does not substitute for evidence, especially when the historical base rate favors FOR.

## Scoring the contested points

| Issue | Edge | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Does history transfer to AI? | Slight AGAINST | The disanalogy is real, but the computerization counterexample is unaddressed, so it is close to a wash |
| Demand elasticity | Neither | Both assert, neither supplies data; the ATM reversal hurts FOR |
| Productivity vs. headcount | Neither | Both infer without data |
| Scale of created vs. eliminated | Slight FOR | AGAINST's comparison is mismatched; FOR's sources are projections |
| Observed evidence to date | Slight AGAINST | Narrow but at least present; FOR offers none |
| Fit to the resolution's wording (net count) | FOR | AGAINST's strongest points are distributional |

## Overall assessment
- **Logic:** FOR is internally consistent but leans on analogy at the key step. AGAINST finds the right crack (cognition) but widens it with unsupported empirical claims and drifts onto adjacent questions.
- **Evidence:** both use real, accurately cited studies for their anchor points. Neither provides sector-level elasticity, hiring or headcount data, which is what would settle the debate.
- **Persuasiveness:** AGAINST is more vivid and the entry-level ladder point lingers. FOR is more measured and better matched to the actual claim.

The lean to FOR is driven by the base rate and by AGAINST's failure to engage the computerization precedent and the net-aggregate framing. It would flip if sector data showed inelastic demand together with falling post-2023 headcount in exposed occupations, particularly at entry level.
