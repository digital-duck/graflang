ARGUMENT FOR THE CLAIM

**1. History shows technology creates more work than it destroys.**
Every major wave of automation, from the mechanized loom to the tractor to the computer, triggered the same fear of mass unemployment, and each time the fear proved wrong. In 1900 roughly 40% of US workers were in agriculture; today it is under 2%, yet employment is at record highs. Economist David Autor and colleagues found that about 60% of US jobs in 2018 had job titles that did not exist in 1940. Software developer, data scientist, UX designer, and social media manager were not on anyone's forecast a few decades earlier. AI is a general-purpose technology on the scale of electricity or the computer, and the pattern points to net creation.

**2. Cheaper production expands demand, and demand creates jobs.**
When AI lowers the cost of a good or service, prices fall, real incomes rise, and people spend the savings elsewhere. ATMs are the classic case: they were expected to wipe out bank tellers, but by making branches cheaper to run, they let banks open more branches, and teller employment in the US actually rose for decades afterward. The same logic applies to AI-assisted legal review, medical imaging, software, and design. When a task becomes ten times cheaper, markets for it that never existed become viable, and the work required to serve them grows.

**3. AI augments workers more than it replaces them, because jobs are bundles of tasks.**
Occupations are made of many tasks, and AI automates some while leaving others, such as judgment, relationships, physical presence, and accountability, intact. Studies of generative AI in the workplace point to augmentation: Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond found that customer-support agents using an AI assistant became about 14% more productive on average, with the largest gains for the least experienced. Higher productivity per worker makes each worker more valuable and lets firms grow rather than shrink. A radiologist who reads scans faster serves more patients; the profession did not vanish when image-recognition AI arrived, and demand for radiologists has stayed strong.

**4. AI builds an entirely new industrial ecosystem.**
Beyond the jobs it changes, AI directly creates large new sectors: data-center construction and operation, chip fabrication, power generation and grid expansion, model development, evaluation, safety, and the integration work of fitting AI into every business. Each of these employs electricians, engineers, technicians, and project managers well beyond the software roles people usually picture. The World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs* reports have projected that job creation from technology and related shifts outpaces displacement in net terms, with AI and data roles among the fastest growing.

**5. Lower barriers to entry unleash entrepreneurship.**
AI hands a single person capabilities that once required a team: drafting contracts, building software, producing marketing, analyzing data. This lowers the cost of starting a business, and new businesses are the main engine of net job creation. A wave of solo founders and small firms, each able to compete with much larger incumbents, will hire as they succeed. The result is a broader base of employers, not a narrower one.

**Conclusion:** The claim rests on a consistent economic mechanism: technology raises productivity, productivity lowers costs and raises incomes, and higher incomes and cheaper goods generate new demand and new kinds of work. AI is a more powerful instance of that mechanism, and the evidence from two centuries of automation says the net result will be more jobs, not fewer.
