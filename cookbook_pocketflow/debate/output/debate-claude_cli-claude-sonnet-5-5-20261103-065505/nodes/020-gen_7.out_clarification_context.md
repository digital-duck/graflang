# What still prevents a verdict

## Contested points that decide the outcome

1. **Does the historical pattern hold when the machine competes for the new tasks too?**
   - FOR (point 1) infers net creation from past waves. AGAINST (point 1) says AI targets cognition, the domain where displaced workers previously landed.
   - Neither side tests this. FOR never explains why new job categories won't also be automatable. AGAINST never answers the precedent of computerization absorbing clerical cognitive work.
   - *Needed:* FOR must name concrete task categories AI is unlikely to absorb in the next decade and show why. AGAINST must explain why computerization of typing, bookkeeping and switchboard work differs from AI, with evidence beyond assertion.

2. **Demand elasticity in exposed sectors.**
   - FOR's mechanism (cheaper output, more demand, more jobs) holds only if demand is elastic. AGAINST asserts it is not ("people don't buy ten times more legal summaries"). Neither gives sector data.
   - *Needed:* elasticity estimates or demand-growth evidence for customer support, coding, translation, legal and administrative work. The ATM case is also unresolved. FOR should address why teller employment fell after about 2010.

3. **Productivity versus headcount.**
   - FOR's 14% productivity result shows output per worker rose, not that employment did. AGAINST's claim that firms capture savings as margin or headcount cuts is equally unevidenced.
   - *Needed:* firm- or sector-level data on whether AI adoption raised or lowered hiring.

4. **Scale of created versus eliminated jobs.**
   - AGAINST compares IMF *exposure* (which includes complemented jobs) with an unsourced *gross* creation figure. FOR cites WEF employer surveys, which are projections, not measurements.
   - *Needed:* a like-for-like net estimate (gross displaced versus gross created, with indirect effects) over a stated horizon, with sources and uncertainty ranges.

5. **Observed evidence to date.**
   - AGAINST's freelance-market studies cover narrow segments. FOR offers no post-2023 employment data.
   - *Needed:* hiring, headcount and wage trends for AI-exposed occupations versus comparable unexposed ones, especially entry-level roles.

## Definitional gaps

- **Time horizon.** A five-year and a fifty-year reading could give opposite answers. Neither side states one.
- **Meaning of "jobs".** Raw count or count weighted by quality, wages and durability? AGAINST leans on quality and precarity, while the claim reads as a count.
- **Aggregate versus distributional.** AGAINST concedes that jobs may balance in aggregate while the people hired differ from those displaced. The debaters need to agree whether that concession defeats the claim or only shows transition harm.

## Unsubstantiated claims to support or withdraw

- **FOR:** solo founders and small firms will drive net hiring (point 5, no data); ecosystem jobs (data centers, chips) are large enough to offset losses, and not mostly temporary construction (point 4).
- **AGAINST:** created roles number only in the "thousands to low millions"; data-labeling jobs will be automated within a few years; gains concentrate in capital owners enough to suppress broad demand.

## What would let me decide

Each side should (a) commit to a horizon and a definition of "jobs", (b) supply a sourced net estimate or sector-level elasticity and hiring data for at least two heavily exposed occupations, and (c) rebut the other's strongest point. For FOR, that is the cognition-versus-muscle break. For AGAINST, it is the computerization precedent and the claim's focus on net totals.

If the evidence favors elastic demand in exposed sectors, FOR should win. If it shows inelastic demand and falling post-2023 headcount, AGAINST should. Absent that data, the verdict stays at a narrow, low-confidence lean to FOR.
