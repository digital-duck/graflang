# Judge's Evaluation

## Verdict
**Narrow edge to FOR, low confidence.** The edge comes mainly from how the resolution is worded and from burden allocation, not from FOR proving its case. The prior-round assessment (about 21 to 19) seems fair to me, and I'd keep it.

## What the resolution asks
"More jobs than it eliminates" is a net-count claim, and neither side fixed a horizon. This helps FOR in two ways:
- AGAINST's best material (entry-level erosion, reskilling friction, precarity) is distributional. It shows that *who* loses jobs matters, not that the *count* falls.
- AGAINST concedes the point in its own text: "Even if jobs eventually balance in aggregate, the people eliminated are not the people hired." That is an argument about harm, not about the resolution.

## Argument FOR

**Strengths**
- It has a coherent causal mechanism: productivity, then lower prices and higher incomes, then new demand and new work.
- Its citations are mostly real and accurately used. Autor's finding that about 60% of 2018 job titles did not exist in 1940, the Brynjolfsson et al. 14% productivity result, and the agriculture share are all sound.
- The task-bundle point (point 3) is conceptually strong. Exposure to automation is not the same as elimination.

**Weaknesses**
- **Base-rate transfer is asserted, not defended.** It never explains why AI resembles electricity or computers more than it differs from them. It also never says where displaced *cognitive* workers go.
- **The ATM example is cherry-picked.** Teller employment did rise for decades, but it fell after about 2010, and FOR doesn't address the reversal.
- **A productivity gain is not a headcount gain.** The 14% figure shows augmentation, not that firms hire more. Points 3 and 5 slide from "more valuable per worker" to "firms grow."
- **Point 4 (AI infrastructure) is unquantified.** It doesn't separate durable jobs from construction-phase ones, and the WEF figures are employer surveys, not measurements.
- **Point 5 (solo founders) is speculative.** It offers no evidence that small firms net-hire.
- **It has no post-2023 data**, even though the debate is happening in late 2026.

## Argument AGAINST

**Strengths**
- The point that AI targets cognition, where earlier displaced workers retrained, is a real and serious structural argument.
- The entry-level and apprenticeship-pipeline point is the most original and forward-looking claim on either side.
- The China-shock evidence is used well to show that adjustment friction is real and lasting, and the IMF figures are accurate.
- Its burden-of-proof framing is rhetorically effective.

**Weaknesses**
- **It never addresses computerization.** That is its biggest gap. Computers automated clerical and cognitive work (typing, bookkeeping, switchboards) without net job loss. AGAINST needs a mechanism for why AI differs, such as diffusion speed, generality, or the ability to automate newly created tasks, and it only asserts the difference.
- **Exposure is not elimination.** Setting IMF exposure (about 40%) against an unsourced "thousands to low millions" of created jobs is not a like-for-like comparison, because exposure includes jobs AI complements.
- **The inelastic-demand claim is asserted without sector data.** "People don't buy ten times more legal summaries" is intuition. Coding and other sectors could well be elastic.
- **The evidence is thin and early.** The freelance-market writing and translation results are narrow and cover only the first months after generative models appeared, so they don't show economy-wide displacement.
- **Its own framing undercuts it.** It claims the question is about aggregate net jobs, then argues mostly about distribution and timing.
- **Point 5 assumes firms bank all savings as margin.** It ignores the competitive pressure that pushes firms to pass savings on or expand.
- **It uses no post-2023 aggregate data**, so it is equally exposed on recency.

## Head-to-head

| Dimension | FOR | AGAINST |
|---|---|---|
| Logical structure | Coherent but leans on analogy | Coherent but partly shifts the question |
| Evidence quality | Real but selective, with the ATM reversal omitted | Real but narrow, with exposure conflated with loss |
| Handling the opponent | Doesn't engage the "cognition" challenge | Doesn't engage computerization |
| Fit to the resolution | Good (net count) | Weaker (mostly distributional) |
| Novel insight | Task bundles, augmentation | Entry-level pipeline, adjustment friction |

## Why FOR edges ahead
1. The wording favors FOR: the claim is about net count, and AGAINST's best arguments are about distribution.
2. Both sides' arguments rest on the historical base rate, and the burden of showing a break from it falls on AGAINST. AGAINST gives a plausible reason for a break (cognition) but no mechanism or data.
3. AGAINST's rebuttal of the historical pattern omits the closest precedent, computerization.

## What would change the outcome
- **Toward AGAINST:** post-2023 declines in headcount and hiring in exposed occupations (especially entry-level), evidence of inelastic demand in major sectors, and a specific reason computerization doesn't apply.
- **Toward FOR:** sector data showing exposed occupations holding or growing employment, a concrete account of where displaced cognitive work migrates, and an answer to the ATM reversal.

Without that evidence, the net-count wording and the historical base rate keep this a low-confidence lean to FOR.
