# Open Questions Blocking a Verdict

**Status:** The 21 to 19 lean to FOR stands, but it rests on the wording of the resolution and on who carries the burden, not on demonstrated outcomes. Six issues keep it from being decisive.

## 1. The horizon and the counting rule are undefined
- Neither side says over what period "will create more than it eliminates" is measured: 5 years, 20 years, or indefinitely.
- Neither says whether "jobs" means headcount, hours, or wage-weighted employment, or whether it counts gross flows or net change.
- This decides the outcome. AGAINST's friction and transition arguments carry weight over a 5 to 10 year window and almost none over 50 years. FOR's historical mechanism works the other way around.
- **Needed:** Each side must state a horizon and a metric, and argue for the claim under that definition. AGAINST must say whether it is contesting the net count or conceding it and arguing harm.

## 2. FOR never shows that AI belongs in the historical reference class
- FOR asserts that AI resembles electricity and computers, but never says where displaced cognitive workers go.
- AGAINST asserts a break from history (AI targets cognition) but gives no mechanism or data.
- Computerization automated clerical and cognitive work without net job loss. AGAINST ignores this precedent, so its "no landing zone" claim is untested.
- **Needed from FOR:** a concrete account of which tasks or sectors absorb displaced cognitive workers.
- **Needed from AGAINST:** a specific reason computerization is not a valid precedent. Candidates are diffusion speed, generality, or AI automating newly created tasks as fast as they appear.

## 3. There is no recent aggregate evidence from either side
- It is late 2026, and neither side cites post-2023 data on employment, hiring, or unemployment in exposed occupations.
- FOR's evidence is historical or from early studies (the 14% productivity result). AGAINST's is a narrow freelance-market finding from the first months after generative models appeared. Neither establishes an economy-wide effect.
- **Needed:** Employment and hiring trends for exposed versus non-exposed occupations, split by seniority, and the source of each figure.

## 4. Exposure, augmentation, and elimination are conflated
- AGAINST sets IMF exposure (about 40%) against an unsourced "thousands to low millions" of new jobs. Exposure includes jobs AI complements, so the comparison is not like-for-like.
- FOR moves from "each worker is more productive" to "firms hire more" without evidence. A productivity gain does not guarantee a headcount gain.
- **Needed:** Estimates of how much exposure becomes substitution rather than complementarity, and evidence on whether firms use gains to expand or to cut headcount.

## 5. Demand elasticity is the pivotal premise, and neither side has data
- FOR's points 2 and 5 require elastic demand for AI-affected goods. AGAINST's point 5 requires inelastic demand.
- Both are intuition. AGAINST's "no one buys ten times more legal summaries" is untested, and software and other sectors may behave very differently.
- **Needed:** Sector-level elasticity evidence for the main exposed industries, or at least a defended argument for which sectors fall on each side.

## 6. Specific claims need support or a response
- **FOR's ATM example:** Teller employment fell after about 2010. FOR has not addressed the reversal.
- **FOR's point 4 (AI infrastructure jobs):** There is no quantification, and no distinction between durable operating jobs and temporary construction jobs.
- **FOR's point 5 (solo founders as net hirers):** No evidence is given that these firms hire on net.
- **AGAINST's claim that firms bank savings as margin:** It ignores competitive pass-through of savings to prices.
- **AGAINST's entry-level and apprenticeship-pipeline argument:** It is the strongest original claim in the debate, but it needs data. It also needs a link to the resolution: does losing the pipeline reduce the net job count, or only its composition?

## What would allow a decision
- **To move toward AGAINST:** recent data showing falling hiring or headcount in exposed occupations, especially entry-level; evidence of inelastic demand in major sectors; a rebuttal of computerization as a precedent; and an argument that the distributional harms reduce the net count rather than just reshuffling it.
- **To move toward FOR:** sector data showing exposed occupations holding or growing; a named destination for displaced cognitive workers; an answer to the ATM reversal; and evidence that productivity gains translate into hiring.
- **For either side to win outright:** agree on the horizon and the metric first. Without that, the two cases are arguing about different questions.
