# Judge's Evaluation

## Argument FOR

**Strengths**
- **Verifiable evidence.** The Autor et al. finding (about 60% of 2018 employment in job titles that didn't exist in 1940) and the Brynjolfsson-Li-Raymond result (about 14% productivity gain, largest for novices) are accurately cited and relevant.
- **A clear causal mechanism.** The chain of productivity, lower prices, higher real income, and new demand is standard economics, and the ATM and radiology cases illustrate it concretely.
- **It targets the actual claim.** It argues about aggregate job counts, which is what the resolution asks.

**Weaknesses**
- **Reasoning by analogy.** Its core support is "it happened every time before," and it doesn't engage with why AI might differ. Calling AI a general-purpose technology like electricity asserts the similarity rather than showing it.
- **Selective examples.** Teller employment did rise for decades, but it declined after roughly 2010, which the argument omits. Radiology is one profession still in demand, not evidence of the economy-wide net.
- **Weak points 4 and 5.** Data centers and chip fabs are capital-intensive and employ relatively few people per dollar invested, and construction jobs are temporary. The solo-founder boom is speculation. WEF figures are employer surveys, not measurements.
- **No treatment of transition.** It never addresses who gets the new jobs or how fast.

## Argument AGAINST

**Strengths**
- **A real structural challenge.** The point that AI targets cognition, the sector that absorbed displaced workers before, is the strongest case against simple extrapolation from history.
- **The entry-level and apprenticeship point.** This is a substantive, non-obvious argument supported by early evidence, such as the freelance-market studies. It also notes that aggregate counts can hide structural damage.
- **Good use of the China shock research.** It shows that adjustment frictions are real and long-lasting.
- **The elasticity objection.** Whether demand expands enough to absorb productivity gains is the right empirical question, and it varies by sector.

**Weaknesses**
- **Drift from the resolution.** Points 3 and 4 are mostly about distribution and transition costs. The argument itself concedes that jobs may "balance in aggregate" while "the people eliminated are not the people hired." That is a serious concern, but it doesn't rebut *more jobs than eliminated*.
- **Exposure is not elimination.** The IMF's 40% and 60% figures include jobs where AI complements workers. Comparing them against gross new-role counts is apples to oranges.
- **Unsupported numbers.** "Thousands to low millions" of new roles is asserted without a source, and it ignores indirect and induced employment.
- **Overstated historical contrast.** Computers already automated large amounts of cognitive clerical work (typists, bookkeepers, switchboard operators), so "past revolutions only automated muscle" is too strong.
- **Speculative claims.** "No obvious landing zone," inelastic demand, and gains concentrating in capital owners are plausible but unproven.
- **Burden-of-proof framing.** Declaring that the other side bears the burden is a rhetorical move, not evidence. Both sides are forecasting.

## Overall Assessment

Both sides argue from incomplete evidence, because the claim is a forecast. FOR offers an empirical record and a coherent mechanism but under-argues why this time is not different. AGAINST offers the better case for novelty and for painful transitions, but much of it addresses a different question (who is hurt and how quickly) than the one posed (net job count).

**Verdict: a narrow edge to the FOR side.** Its evidence is more concrete and its argument stays on the resolution. AGAINST wins decisively on the narrower point that displacement could be fast, concentrated, and hard to reverse for specific groups, particularly entry-level workers. A stronger version of either side would improve on its weak point:
- **FOR** would need to explain why the cognitive-automation difference doesn't break the historical pattern.
- **AGAINST** would need to show that elasticity is too low to offset displacement in aggregate, not just that transitions are painful.
