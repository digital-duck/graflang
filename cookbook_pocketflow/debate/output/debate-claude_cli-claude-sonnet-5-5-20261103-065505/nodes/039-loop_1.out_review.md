# Judgment: narrow edge to FOR

The edge comes from the wording of the resolution, which concerns net job counts. It does not come from either side proving its case.

## Argument FOR

**Strengths**
- **A coherent mechanism.** Productivity gains lower costs, raise incomes, and create new demand and new tasks. It is the standard economic account, and it ties the five points together.
- **Verifiable evidence.** The Autor et al. finding on new job titles, the ATM and teller case, and the Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond customer-support study are all real and reasonably described.
- **It fits the resolution.** The claim is about net job counts, and the historical record on net counts favors FOR.
- **Task-bundle reasoning.** The idea that AI automates tasks rather than whole occupations is more precise than AGAINST's treatment of "jobs."

**Weaknesses**
- **The reference class is assumed.** Treating AI as electricity or the computer is the key move, and FOR never says where displaced cognitive workers go. Naming concrete destination sectors would have answered AGAINST's best point.
- **The ATM example is incomplete.** Teller employment fell after about 2010, and FOR does not address the reversal. The example is also about complementarity, which is the contested question.
- **Unsupported steps.** Moving from productivity per worker to firm growth and headcount growth is asserted, not shown. Point 4 (infrastructure jobs) is not quantified and does not separate temporary construction work from durable operating jobs. Point 5 (solo founders as net hirers) has no evidence behind it.
- **No recent data.** It is late 2026, and nothing after the early generative-AI studies is cited.

## Argument AGAINST

**Strengths**
- **The cognition point is a real argument.** If AI is general across cognitive work, the refuge that absorbed past displacement is smaller. It forces FOR to justify the analogy.
- **The entry-level pipeline claim is the strongest original point in the debate.** It identifies a structural harm that headcount totals can hide.
- **Transition frictions are real.** Age, geography, and credentials slow reallocation, and the China-shock literature shows slow adjustment.
- **It challenges the demand-elasticity premise.** FOR's mechanism does depend on elastic demand.

**Weaknesses**
- **It mismatches the resolution.** Many of its points concern distribution, transition costs, and who gets hired. The claim is about the aggregate net count. The argument concedes in places that aggregates may balance ("even if jobs eventually balance...") and then argues harm, which shifts the question.
- **Exposure is treated as elimination.** The IMF's 40% figure includes jobs AI complements. Comparing it to an unsourced "thousands to low millions" of new roles is not like-for-like.
- **The China-shock analogy is weak.** It was a trade shock, not a technology shock. It supports slow adjustment but not net job loss.
- **The margin claim ignores competition.** Firms in competitive markets pass savings into prices, which feeds the demand mechanism FOR relies on.
- **The elasticity point is intuition.** "No one buys ten times more legal summaries" is untested, and demand for software and other outputs may behave very differently.
- **It shifts the burden without discharging it.** It says the burden lies with FOR, but it offers no data on actual displacement beyond one narrow freelance-market finding.
- **The "no landing zone" claim is untested.** It ignores that computerization automated clerical and cognitive work without net job loss, and it gives no specific reason that precedent fails here.

## Comparison

| Criterion | FOR | AGAINST |
|---|---|---|
| Logical coherence | Strong mechanism, but unsupported steps | Good break-from-history thesis, but drifts to distribution |
| Evidence quality | Real but mostly historical or early | Real but narrow, with mismatched comparisons |
| Fit to the resolution | Direct | Partial |
| Handling of the opponent | Does not answer the cognition objection | Does not answer the computerization precedent |
| Persuasive force | Steady, base-rate driven | Vivid, with the pipeline point the standout |

## Reasoning for the verdict

Both sides leave their pivotal premise unproven. FOR needs AI to behave like past general-purpose technologies. AGAINST needs it to break from them. Absent decisive evidence, the historical base rate carries more weight than an untested claim of a break. That tips the balance to FOR, but only slightly.

The result is also sensitive to two things neither side defined: the time horizon and the metric. Over a 5 to 10 year window, AGAINST's friction arguments gain force. Over several decades, FOR's mechanism does. If the question were whether AI's transition will be painful or unequal, AGAINST would likely win.

## What would change the outcome

- **Toward AGAINST:** post-2023 data showing falling hiring or headcount in exposed occupations, especially entry-level; evidence of inelastic demand in major exposed sectors; and a specific reason computerization is not a valid precedent.
- **Toward FOR:** data showing exposed occupations holding or growing, named destination sectors for displaced cognitive workers, and an answer to the ATM reversal.
