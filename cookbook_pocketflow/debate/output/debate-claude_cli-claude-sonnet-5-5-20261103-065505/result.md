# Verdict: FOR wins (21 to 18)

**Claim:** "Artificial intelligence will create more jobs than it eliminates"

## Decisive arguments

**1. FOR addressed the resolution as written.** The claim is about net job count. FOR's case (historical reallocation, demand expansion, new sectors, new firms) all bears on that quantity. AGAINST's strongest points are about *who* loses work and *how painfully*, which are distribution and transition questions. Those matter, but they don't show that the aggregate number goes negative.

**2. FOR's evidence is concrete and checkable.** The agriculture shift (about 40% to under 2% of workers with record employment), Autor's finding that about 60% of 2018 job titles didn't exist in 1940, the ATM and teller case, and the 14% productivity gain in customer support are specific data points. AGAINST leaned more on projections and analogies.

**3. AGAINST's comparison was mismatched.** It set the *exposed* jobs (IMF: about 40% of global employment) against the *new* AI-specific roles (ML engineers, prompt specialists). Exposure is not elimination, since exposed jobs are often augmented. And AI-titled roles are only a slice of what is created, because FOR's demand-expansion and infrastructure channels create jobs in electrical work, construction, and downstream industries. This weakened AGAINST's central quantitative claim.

**4. AGAINST's best points were real but did not carry the resolution.** The entry-level erosion, the reskilling friction (the China shock research), and the argument that demand may be inelastic in some sectors are credible and should inform policy. They show the transition may be harsh and uneven. They fall short of proving net destruction.

## Where both fell short

The whole debate turns on one premise neither side proved: whether AI is *another* general-purpose technology that follows the historical pattern, or a *break* from it because it targets cognition itself. FOR assumed continuity, and AGAINST asserted discontinuity. Neither offered decisive evidence, and no one can yet, since the long-run outcome is still unobserved. FOR also underplayed transition costs, and AGAINST's "no landing zone" argument was speculative.

## Final verdict

**FOR wins, narrowly.** On the question actually posed, net job creation versus elimination, FOR's historically grounded mechanism is better supported than AGAINST's argument from novelty. The margin is thin, because the outcome depends on whether the historical pattern holds for AI, and that remains unresolved. AGAINST's points on entry-level displacement and the pace of adjustment should be read as caveats on *how* the net gain would be experienced, not as refutation of it.
